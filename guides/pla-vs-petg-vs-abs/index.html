PLA vs PETG vs ABS: Which Filament Should You Actually Use?

A practical, no-nonsense comparison of PLA, PETG, and ABS filament. When to use each, what they cost, and the mistakes that waste your money and filament.
May 20, 2026 · 4 min · Luca

There are dozens of 3D printing filaments, but 95% of what you print should be one of three: PLA, PETG, or ABS. Pick the wrong one and you get warped parts, failed prints, or objects that melt in a hot car. Here’s how to choose without wasting spools learning the hard way.

The 10-Second Answer#

  • PLA — your default. Easy to print, cheap, looks great. Use it unless you have a specific reason not to.
  • PETG — when the part needs to survive heat, moisture, or impact. Outdoor and functional parts.
  • ABS — when you need real toughness and high heat resistance, and you have an enclosure.

If you’re new: start with PLA. Don’t overthink it. You can learn the others later.

PLA — The Default#

PLA (polylactic acid) is the easiest filament to print and the reason most people succeed on their first try. Low printing temperature, minimal warping, no enclosure needed.

Use PLA for:

  • Decorative objects, models, miniatures
  • Prototypes and test prints
  • Indoor functional parts that won’t see heat
  • Anything where appearance matters (it has the best surface finish)

Avoid PLA for:

  • Anything left in a hot car (it softens around 60°C — a sunny dashboard will deform it)
  • Outdoor parts exposed to sun
  • High-stress mechanical parts (it’s stiff but brittle)

Cost: Cheapest tier. ~$15-25 per 1kg spool.

Print difficulty: Easiest. Works at stock settings on nearly every printer.

PETG — The Practical Workhorse#

PETG (polyethylene terephthalate glycol) is the “I need this to actually work” filament. It’s tougher than PLA, handles heat and moisture far better, and is more impact-resistant. The tradeoff is it’s slightly fussier to print — stringing is the main annoyance.

Use PETG for:

  • Functional parts that flex without snapping
  • Outdoor items (planters, brackets, mounts)
  • Anything exposed to water or moderate heat
  • Food-adjacent items (use food-grade PETG and your own judgment)

Avoid PETG for:

  • Highly detailed miniatures (stringing hurts fine detail)
  • Situations where you want a perfect glossy finish without tuning

The tuning gotcha: PETG needs a slower first layer, a higher bed temperature, and retraction tuning to control stringing. Budget one calibration pass before you trust it.

Cost: Slightly more than PLA. ~$20-30 per 1kg spool.

Print difficulty: Moderate. Worth the small learning curve.

ABS — The Tough One (With Conditions)#

ABS (acrylonitrile butadiene styrene) is what Lego is made of. It’s tough, machinable, and handles high heat. It’s also the hardest of the three to print well, because it warps badly without a controlled environment.

Use ABS for:

  • Automotive parts (it survives a hot car where PLA dies)
  • Enclosures and housings that need durability
  • Parts you’ll drill, tap, or post-process with acetone smoothing

Avoid ABS if:

  • You don’t have an enclosure. ABS warps and cracks in open air and drafts.
  • You print in a poorly ventilated room — ABS fumes are unpleasant and you want airflow management.

The hard requirement: An enclosure (even a DIY closet or box) and ambient temperature control. Without it, large ABS prints will lift off the bed and crack. This is non-negotiable.

Cost: Similar to PETG. ~$20-30 per 1kg spool.

Print difficulty: Hardest of the three. Don’t start here.

The Mistakes That Waste Money#

  1. Buying ABS as a beginner. It will warp, you’ll get frustrated, and you’ll blame the printer. Start with PLA.
  2. Using PLA for a car phone mount. It will deform on the first sunny day. Use PETG or ABS.
  3. Ignoring filament storage. PETG and especially nylon absorb moisture from the air, which causes popping and poor layer adhesion. A cheap sealed bin with desiccant solves it.
  4. Not doing a tuning pass per material. Each filament wants different temps and retraction. The “one profile for everything” approach produces mediocre prints across the board.

What to Buy First#

If you own a printer and need a starting kit:

  • 2 spools of PLA in colors you like — this covers most of what you’ll print early on.
  • 1 spool of PETG for when you make something that needs to survive the real world.
  • Skip ABS until you have an enclosure and a specific need.

Bottom Line#

PLA for looks and ease. PETG for function and the outdoors. ABS for heat and toughness when you have an enclosure. Most people never need anything else — and the ones who do already know it.
